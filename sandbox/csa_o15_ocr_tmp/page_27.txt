The fibre stresses are stresses for the purposes of classification and are close approximations to the average bending strengths (MOR) specified in Table
A.I for poles 20 to 35 ft in length.
The bending strengths specified in Table A.I are derived from specimens whose moisture contents at the groundline were above the fibre saturation point
at the time of testing to failure. This condition is recognized as the best approximation for the purposes of this Standard. Furthermore, the average moduli
of elasticity specified in this Table are derived from poles of any length that were unseasoned or completely submerged in water for some time before
testing, as described in the publications referenced in Clause A.9.
The strength of poles longer than 50 ft can be less, as indicated in Annex A.
Fibre stress for several species was based on limited test information or results from one test site; therefore, the Subcommittee is uncertain of the
adequacy of the pole dimensions for these species and recommends that further tests be performed to supplement the database. Notes concerning fibre
stress accompany Tables 3 and E.3.
Other criteria related to the classification of poles are as follows:
a) The taper for determining the circumference at the classification point should be the difference between the minimum top circumference and the
circumference at the theoretical groundline, divided by the length between them.
b) The loading point should be O. 61 m (2 ft) from the pole top.
c) The classification point should be 1.83 m (6 ft) from the butt.
d) The term "class" as used in this Standard does not refer to quality limitations or defects ofa pole or reinforcing stub; it is used to differentiate poles
and stubs on the basis of their potential force-resisting capabilities.
Pole test data for yellow cedar, eastern white pine, and ponderosa pine were not available; hence, the values specified in this Table approximate the
average strengths of small, clear bending specimens, as described in the publications referenced in Clause A.9.
Samples of Douglas fir poles taken from coastal areas of British Columbia and from the Cascade Mountains region of Oregon have significantly higher
average strengths than those taken from Rocky Mountain or interior areas. Thus, only Douglas fir poles taken from the western slopes of the Coast and
Cascade Mountains (as defined by the Atlas of Canada and the National Atlas of the United States) shall be classified as coastal Douglas fir. All other
Douglas fir poles shall be classified as interior Douglas fir.
